📘 1 · Washington Manual of Critical Care

Non-Invasive Support — Who, and Why

Non-invasive support succeeds or fails on patient selection. Used for the right indication — a COPD exacerbation with respiratory acidosis, or acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema — non-invasive ventilation prevents intubation and saves lives. Used to postpone an inevitable intubation in the wrong patient, it simply delays a definitive airway and worsens the outcome. The skill is knowing which patient is in front of you, and recognising failure early.

Summarised from the Washington Manual of Critical Care — Non-Invasive Ventilation & Respiratory Failure.

Type 1 — Hypoxaemic

  • PaO₂ <8 kPa (60 mmHg), normal/low CO₂
  • Pneumonia, ARDS, oedema
  • Support: O₂ → HFNC → CPAP/NIV → intubate

Type 2 — Hypercapnic

  • PaCO₂ >6 kPa (45 mmHg) + acidosis (pH <7.35)
  • COPD, obesity-hypoventilation, neuromuscular
  • Support: NIV (BiPAP) is first-line

Cardiogenic oedema

  • "Flash" pulmonary oedema
  • CPAP or NIV + nitrates/diuretics
  • Reduces intubation & mortality
🔧 The three tools, in plain terms
  • HFNC (high-flow nasal cannula) — heated humidified O₂ at 30–60 L/min: gives a reliable high FiO₂, washes out CO₂ dead space, provides a little PEEP and is comfortable. Best in type 1 (hypoxaemic) failure.
  • CPAP — continuous pressure (one level): recruits alveoli, raises functional residual capacity, offloads the failing heart. Best in hypoxaemia & cardiogenic oedema.
  • NIV / BiPAP — two pressures (IPAP + EPAP): the difference (pressure support) does the ventilating work and clears CO₂. Best in type 2 (hypercapnic) failure.
📗 2 · Marino's The ICU Book, 5th Edition (2025)

Physiology of Support & of Weaning

Positive pressure applied to the airway does three useful things: it recruits collapsed lung and improves oxygenation, it unloads the fatiguing respiratory muscles, and — in the failing left ventricle — it lowers both preload and afterload. The mirror image explains why weaning fails: the moment support is withdrawn, the work of breathing and the cardiac load both return, and the patient who cannot meet that demand decompensates. Weaning is therefore a cardiorespiratory stress test.

📗 Marino — the rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI)

The RSBI = respiratory rate ÷ tidal volume (in litres), measured during a brief spontaneous trial. A value <105 predicts weaning success reasonably well; a high RSBI (fast, small breaths) signals load exceeding capacity. It is a guide, not a gate — a good SBT plus an assessment of airway, cough and secretions matters more than any single number.

📗 Marino — separate the ability to breathe from the ability to protect the airway

Passing an SBT means the lungs and pump can cope; it does not guarantee a safe extubation. Before pulling the tube, confirm an adequate cough, manageable secretions, and airway patency (consider a cuff-leak test in those at risk of laryngeal oedema). Weaning failure and extubation failure are different problems with different fixes.

📋 3 · Guidelines & Landmark Trials

Evidence-Based Practice

NIV — strong indications STRONG — ERS/ATS 2017
NIV is recommended for: acute hypercapnic COPD exacerbation with respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35) and acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema. It is also used to facilitate weaning in COPD and for palliation. It is not a substitute for intubation in a patient who needs a definitive airway, and evidence in de novo hypoxaemic failure/pneumonia is weaker — watch such patients extremely closely.
HFNC in hypoxaemic failure FLORALI · HOT trials
In de novo acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, FLORALI (NEJM 2015) found high-flow nasal cannula improved outcomes (including 90-day mortality) versus standard oxygen or NIV, and reduced intubation in the most hypoxaemic. HFNC is comfortable, allows eating/speaking and is a reasonable first step — but, like NIV, its danger is masking deterioration. Reassess frequently and do not let it delay a needed intubation.
Predicting HFNC failure — the ROX index
ROX = (SpO₂/FiO₂) ÷ respiratory rate. Measured at ~2, 6 and 12 h, a ROX ≥4.88 predicts HFNC success; a low or falling ROX flags likely failure and the need to intubate. Use it as a structured re-check, not a reason to persist with a failing trial.
Liberation from the ventilator Daily SAT + SBT
Screen daily for readiness: cause improving, adequate oxygenation on low support (e.g. FiO₂ ≤0.4, PEEP ≤5–8, PaO₂/FiO₂ acceptable), haemodynamic stability off/low vasopressors, awake and able to cough. Pair a spontaneous awakening trial (sedation hold) with a spontaneous breathing trial (30–120 min on low pressure support or T-piece). Extubate a patient who passes — and give prophylactic NIV or HFNC after extubation to high-risk patients (COPD, hypercapnia, heart failure) to prevent re-intubation.
🇮🇳 Indian Context

NIV and HFNC are workhorses that conserve scarce ICU ventilators and beds — invaluable in COPD exacerbations and cardiogenic oedema, and heavily used during respiratory surges. Where oxygen supply and blender capacity are limited, remember HFNC and NIV can consume large volumes of oxygen; plan supply. The greatest local pitfall is over-reliance on non-invasive support in hypoxaemic pneumonia without close monitoring — set explicit time-limited trials with clear failure criteria (worsening gas exchange, low/falling ROX, exhaustion, altered sensorium) and a pre-agreed plan to intubate, so a delayed intubation does not become a crash intubation.

🗺 5 · Support & Weaning Flowchart

From Failure to Liberation

1

Classify the failure

  • ABG: type 1 (hypoxaemic) vs type 2 (hypercapnic + acidotic)
  • Identify & treat the cause (COPD, oedema, pneumonia, neuromuscular)
  • Exclude an immediate need for intubation (exhaustion, ↓GCS, unstable, can't protect airway)
2

Choose the tool

  • COPD acidosis / hypercapnia → NIV (BiPAP); cardiogenic oedema → CPAP/NIV + nitrates/diuretics
  • De novo hypoxaemia → HFNC (± CPAP); treat the pneumonia/ARDS
3

Set a time-limited trial with failure criteria

  • Recheck at 1–2h: ABG (NIV), ROX (HFNC), work of breathing, sensorium
  • Not improving → intubate — do not persist; a delayed intubation is dangerous
4

Once ventilated — screen daily to liberate

  • Cause improving, oxygenating on low support, stable, awake, coughing
  • Pair sedation hold (SAT) + spontaneous breathing trial (SBT)
5

Extubate & support

  • Confirm cough, secretions, airway (cuff-leak if at risk) before extubation
  • Prophylactic NIV/HFNC for high-risk patients; watch for post-extubation stridor/failure
⚠️ 6 · Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes with Non-Invasive Support & Weaning

❌ Mistake 1 — Using NIV to delay an inevitable intubation

In a patient who is exhausted, obtunded, unstable or cannot protect the airway, non-invasive support postpones a definitive airway and worsens outcome. Set explicit failure criteria and intubate when they are met.

❌ Mistake 2 — Wrong tool for the failure type

HFNC/CPAP don't ventilate — they won't clear CO₂ in a hypercapnic COPD patient, who needs the pressure-support (IPAP–EPAP difference) of BiPAP. Conversely, BiPAP isn't first-line for de novo hypoxaemia.

❌ Mistake 3 — Not rechecking an ABG on NIV

Recheck gases at ~1 hour: if the pH/CO₂ isn't improving, the trial is failing. "It looks better" is not enough in respiratory acidosis.

❌ Mistake 4 — Ignoring the ROX index on HFNC

A low or falling ROX (<4.88) predicts HFNC failure. Use it as a scheduled checkpoint at 2/6/12h rather than watching comfort alone.

❌ Mistake 5 — No daily weaning screen / deep sedation

Patients stay ventilated too long when readiness isn't screened daily and sedation isn't held. Pair a spontaneous awakening trial with an SBT to shorten ventilation.

❌ Mistake 6 — Confusing weaning failure with extubation failure

Passing an SBT proves the pump works, not that the airway is safe. Assess cough, secretions and cuff-leak before extubation — otherwise you re-intubate for stridor or sputum retention.

❌ Mistake 7 — Not using prophylactic post-extubation support

High-risk patients (COPD, hypercapnia, heart failure) benefit from NIV/HFNC applied immediately after extubation to prevent re-intubation — reactive rescue NIV after failure works less well.

🎓 7 · Exam Pearls — DrNB / PDCC / IFCCM / EDIC

Exam Pearls

Q: Strongest indications for NIV?
Acute hypercapnic COPD exacerbation with respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35) and acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema — both reduce intubation and mortality (ERS/ATS 2017).

Q: Difference between CPAP and BiPAP?
CPAP delivers one continuous pressure (recruits lung, offloads the heart) and oxygenates; BiPAP delivers two pressures — the IPAP–EPAP difference is pressure support that ventilates and clears CO₂ (for hypercapnic failure).

Q: What did FLORALI show?
In de novo hypoxaemic respiratory failure, HFNC improved outcomes (including 90-day mortality) versus standard O₂ or NIV, and reduced intubation in the most hypoxaemic.

Q: Define the ROX index and its threshold.
ROX = (SpO₂/FiO₂)/respiratory rate; a value ≥4.88 (at 2/6/12h) predicts HFNC success — a low/falling ROX predicts failure.

Q: What is the RSBI and its cut-off?
Rapid shallow breathing index = RR ÷ tidal volume (L); <105 predicts successful weaning. High values mean load exceeds capacity.

Q: How do you perform a spontaneous breathing trial?
30–120 min on low pressure support (5–8) with PEEP 5, or T-piece. Pass = stable RR, SpO₂, HR and BP with no distress; then reassess airway/cough before extubation.

Q: Why can weaning cause pulmonary oedema?
Removing positive intrathoracic pressure raises LV preload and afterload; a patient with cardiac dysfunction develops weaning-induced pulmonary oedema — diurese/optimise and re-trial.

Q: Who gets prophylactic post-extubation NIV/HFNC?
High-risk patients — COPD/hypercapnia, heart failure, older patients — to prevent re-intubation; a cuff-leak test screens those at risk of post-extubation stridor.
