📘 1 · Miller / Morgan & Mikhail — The Problem

Why Tonsillectomy Is a Classic Paediatric Case

"Adenotonsillectomy is one of the commonest paediatric operations, yet it concentrates several hazards into one small patient: a shared airway worked by the surgeon, a child often operated on for obstructive sleep apnoea, a highly vascular field, and the ever-present risk of post-tonsillectomy bleeding returning as a hypovolaemic child with a stomach full of blood."

Synthesised from Miller's Anesthesia & Morgan & Mikhail's Clinical Anesthesiology.
🎯 The core anaesthetic concerns
  • Paediatric patient — infant/child physiology, ↓ FRC, high O₂ consumption, temperature and blood-volume sensitivity.
  • Shared airway — the surgeon and anaesthetist work in the same field; the mouth gag can kink, compress or dislodge the tube.
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) — sensitised to opioids, prone to obstruction and post-op respiratory events; may have pulmonary hypertension.
  • Bleeding — a vascular field, blood loss hard to estimate, and the feared post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage.
  • PONV — one of the most emetogenic paediatric operations.

Indications

IndicationDetail
Obstruction / OSAThe commonest modern indication — adenotonsillar hypertrophy causing obstructive sleep-disordered breathing
Recurrent infection (Paradise criteria)≥7 episodes in 1 year · ≥5/year for 2 years · ≥3/year for 3 years; or >2 weeks of missed school
Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)Not responding to medical management / recurrent
Chronic tonsillitis · Strep carrier · Suspected neoplasmChronic disease, streptococcal carriage, or unilateral enlargement raising malignancy concern

Contraindications

① Deranged coagulation profile · ② Active infection (defer) · ③ Partial/complete cleft lip or palate — risk of velopharyngeal insufficiency after removing the adenoids/tonsils · ④ Hb < 10 g/dL (optimise first).
