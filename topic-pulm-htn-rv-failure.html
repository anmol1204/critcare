📘 1 · Washington Manual of Critical Care

The Ventricle the ICU Forgets

The right ventricle is a thin-walled, volume-pump built for a low-pressure circuit; it tolerates volume well but copes poorly with an acute rise in afterload. When pulmonary vascular resistance climbs suddenly — a massive pulmonary embolism, ARDS, decompensated pulmonary hypertension, or the wrong ventilator settings — the RV dilates, fails, and drags the whole circulation down with it. Managing acute RV failure is the art of respecting three levers at once: right-sided preload, right coronary perfusion, and pulmonary afterload. Get any one badly wrong and the patient arrests.

Summarised from the Washington Manual of Critical Care — Right Ventricular Failure & Pulmonary Hypertension.

Recognise decompensation

  • Raised JVP, hepatomegaly, peripheral oedema, low-output state (cool, hypotensive, oliguric, rising lactate).
  • Echo — dilated, hypokinetic RV, D-shaped septum (flattening in systole = pressure overload), a plethoric IVC, reduced TAPSE, tricuspid regurgitation.
  • ECG — right axis, RBBB, S1Q3T3, right-heart strain; raised troponin/BNP mark strain and prognosis.
  • Precipitants — PE, sepsis, ARDS, arrhythmia (loss of atrial kick is poorly tolerated), fluid overload, missed PH therapy, surgery.
📗 2 · Marino's The ICU Book, 5th Edition (2025)

The Vicious Cycle You Have to Break

Once the right ventricle starts to fail, it enters a self-amplifying spiral. Rising RV pressure and volume bow the interventricular septum into the left ventricle, impairing LV filling and dropping cardiac output and systemic blood pressure. But the right coronary artery perfuses the RV largely in systole and depends on the aortic (systemic) pressure — so as systemic pressure falls, RV coronary perfusion falls, the ischaemic RV fails further, and the cycle tightens. The therapeutic key is to keep the systemic pressure above the RV pressure so the right coronary keeps flowing.

Summarised from Marino PL. The ICU Book, 5th Ed — Acute Right Heart Failure.

📗 Marino — the three levers, and the fluid trap
  • Preload — the failing RV is usually overfilled; a fluid bolus often worsens septal shift. Give fluid only if clearly preload-responsive (small/underfilled RV) — otherwise diurese.
  • Perfusion — restore systemic pressure early with noradrenaline (or vasopressin, which raises systemic without much pulmonary vasoconstriction) to keep the right coronary flowing.
  • Afterload — aggressively correct the pulmonary vasoconstrictors: hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, atelectasis, pain, high PEEP/overdistension; add an inhaled pulmonary vasodilator (nitric oxide, prostacyclin) which dilates the pulmonary bed without dropping systemic pressure.
📋 3 · Breaking the Spiral

Evidence-Based Management

Optimise volume — often diuresis, rarely a bolus STRONG
Assess volume with echo/CVP/response. The congested, dilated RV needs decongestion (diuretics or ultrafiltration), not fluid. Reserve a cautious 250 mL bolus for the clearly underfilled RV, and stop if it does not help or worsens the septal shift. Restore sinus rhythm/atrial kick promptly — the RV depends heavily on it (cardiovert unstable arrhythmias).
Keep the RV perfused — vasopressor first STRONG
Noradrenaline is first-line to maintain a systemic pressure that exceeds RV pressure and preserve right-coronary flow; vasopressin is a useful adjunct (raises systemic vascular resistance with relatively little pulmonary vasoconstriction). Add an inodilator — dobutamine (low dose) or milrinone — to augment RV contractility and lower PVR, watching for systemic hypotension (pair with the vasopressor). Avoid pure vasodilators/systemic hypotension.
Reduce pulmonary afterload — physiology & inhaled vasodilators ESC/ERS 2022
Correct every reversible pulmonary vasoconstrictor: treat hypoxia, avoid hypercapnia and acidosis, recruit atelectasis, control pain/agitation. Use inhaled nitric oxide or inhaled prostacyclin (iloprost/epoprostenol) — selective pulmonary vasodilators that do not drop systemic pressure. In established PAH, do not stop the patient's chronic PAH therapy (endothelin antagonists, PDE5 inhibitors, prostacyclins) and escalate with the PH team; IV epoprostenol for a PH crisis. Manage the underlying cause (thrombolysis/embolectomy for high-risk PE — see Pulmonary Embolism).
Ventilate the RV gently — intubation is high-risk CAUTION
Intubation and positive-pressure ventilation are dangerous in RV failure: induction vasodilates and drops coronary perfusion, and high intrathoracic pressure/overdistension raises PVR — arrest on induction is common. If unavoidable, optimise haemodynamics first, have vasopressors running, use a haemodynamically stable induction (e.g. ketamine/etomidate), and ventilate with low tidal volumes, modest PEEP, and strict avoidance of hypercapnia/hypoxia/acidosis. Escalate to VA-ECMO as a bridge in refractory RV failure/PH crisis where reversible or transplantable.
🇮🇳 Indian Context

The commonest RV-failure substrates in Indian ICUs are massive pulmonary embolism, ARDS, cor pulmonale from COPD/ILD, and rheumatic mitral valve disease (chronic pulmonary hypertension from long-standing mitral stenosis remains common). Untreated obstructive sleep apnoea and high-altitude exposure also contribute. Advanced options — inhaled nitric oxide, ECMO, and expensive PAH-specific drugs — are limited to tertiary centres, so the physiological RV-protective strategy is the highest-yield care available everywhere: keep SpO₂ up, avoid hypercapnia and acidosis, restore rhythm, use noradrenaline early to protect coronary perfusion, and be very cautious about fluids and intubation. Inhaled prostacyclin (iloprost) via nebuliser is a practical selective pulmonary vasodilator where NO is unavailable.

🗺 5 · Clinical Flowchart

Acute RV Failure — The Three Levers

1

Recognise & find the trigger

  • Low-output shock + raised JVP + dilated hypokinetic RV / D-shaped septum on echo
  • Identify & treat the precipitant — PE, sepsis, ARDS, arrhythmia, missed PAH therapy
2

Lever 1 — optimise preload (usually diurese)

  • Congested RV → diuresis/ultrafiltration; cautious 250 mL bolus only if clearly underfilled
  • Restore sinus rhythm / atrial kick — cardiovert unstable arrhythmia
3

Lever 2 — perfuse the RV

  • Noradrenaline (± vasopressin) to keep systemic pressure above RV pressure
  • Add dobutamine/milrinone for contractility (with a vasopressor to protect BP)
4

Lever 3 — reduce afterload

  • Fix hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, atelectasis, pain; inhaled NO/prostacyclin
  • Continue chronic PAH drugs; treat high-risk PE (thrombolysis/embolectomy)
5

Ventilate gently / escalate

  • Avoid intubation if possible; if needed, stable induction, low Vt, modest PEEP, pressors ready
  • VA-ECMO as a bridge in refractory but reversible/transplantable disease
⚠️ 6 · Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes in RV Failure

❌ Mistake 1 — Reflexively fluid-loading the RV

The failing RV is usually over-filled; a bolus worsens septal shift and drops LV output. Most patients need decongestion, not fluid — give a bolus only for a clearly underfilled RV.

❌ Mistake 2 — Letting systemic pressure fall

Right-coronary perfusion depends on systemic pressure exceeding RV pressure. Allowing hypotension starves the RV and tightens the death spiral — start noradrenaline early.

❌ Mistake 3 — Tolerating hypoxia, hypercapnia or acidosis

All three are potent pulmonary vasoconstrictors that raise RV afterload. Aggressively correct them; they are often the reversible tipping point.

❌ Mistake 4 — Casual intubation

Induction and positive-pressure ventilation frequently precipitate arrest in RV failure. Optimise first, use a haemodynamically stable induction, run pressors, and ventilate gently — or avoid intubation.

❌ Mistake 5 — Stopping chronic PAH therapy

Abruptly withdrawing prostacyclin or other PAH drugs can cause rebound crisis. Continue them and involve the PH team.

❌ Mistake 6 — Using a systemic vasodilator to "offload" the RV

Systemic vasodilators drop coronary perfusion pressure and cause collapse. Reduce pulmonary afterload selectively with inhaled agents instead.

🎓 7 · Exam Pearls — DrNB / PDCC / IFCCM / EDIC

Exam Pearls

Q: Describe the RV death spiral.
↑RV afterload → RV dilatation → septal shift → ↓LV filling → ↓systemic BP → ↓right-coronary perfusion → RV ischaemia → worse failure.

Q: What are the three therapeutic levers?
Preload (usually decongest), perfusion (noradrenaline to keep systemic > RV pressure), and afterload (correct hypoxia/hypercapnia/acidosis + inhaled pulmonary vasodilators).

Q: Why is a fluid bolus often harmful?
The failing RV is usually over-filled; more volume worsens septal shift and reduces LV output. Give fluid only if clearly preload-responsive.

Q: Which vasopressors are preferred?
Noradrenaline first-line; vasopressin as an adjunct (raises systemic vascular resistance with little pulmonary vasoconstriction).

Q: Which pulmonary vasodilators avoid systemic hypotension?
Inhaled nitric oxide and inhaled prostacyclin (iloprost/epoprostenol) — selective for the pulmonary bed.

Q: Why is intubation dangerous?
Induction vasodilates and drops coronary perfusion, and positive-pressure ventilation/overdistension raises PVR — arrest on induction is common.

Q: Echo hallmark of RV pressure overload?
A dilated, hypokinetic RV with a D-shaped (flattened) interventricular septum and reduced TAPSE.
