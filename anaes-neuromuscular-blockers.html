🧬 Neuromuscular Blockers & Reversal

Depolarising vs non-depolarising TOF monitoring Sugammadex
📅 Last reviewed July 2026 · Compiled by Dr. Anmol Srivastava Anaesthesia, Emergency Medicine & Critical Care Medicine · Sources: Miller's Anesthesia · Morgan & Mikhail's Clinical Anesthesiology · Stoelting's Pharmacology

"The neuromuscular blocking drugs paralyse but do not anaesthetise. The two commandments follow from that single fact: never paralyse a patient you cannot ventilate, and never let one wake before the block is fully reversed."

⚡ 1 · Neuromuscular Junction Physiology

How a nerve impulse becomes a muscle twitch

Every blocker acts on the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at the motor end-plate. Understanding the normal cycle makes the pharmacology obvious.

1

Synthesis & storage

Acetylcholine (ACh) is made from acetyl-CoA + choline by choline acetyltransferase and stored in vesicles at the nerve terminal.

▼
2

Release

The action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ influx triggers vesicle fusion and ACh release into the cleft.

▼
3

Receptor activation

ACh binds both α-subunits of the postjunctional nicotinic receptor → cation channel opens → end-plate depolarisation → muscle action potential → contraction.

▼
4

Termination

Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft hydrolyses ACh within milliseconds; choline is recycled. The end-plate repolarises, ready for the next impulse.

The pharmacological levers: non-depolarisers compete at the receptor (block step 3); suxamethonium over-stimulates it; anticholinesterases (neostigmine) raise ACh by blocking step 4; sugammadex removes the drug from the plasma entirely.

🧬 2 · Classification

Two mechanisms, two chemical families

ClassMechanismExamples
DepolarisingReceptor agonist — sustained depolarisation → fasciculation then flaccid paralysisSuxamethonium (succinylcholine)
Non-depolarising — aminosteroidCompetitive antagonist at the receptorRocuronium, Vecuronium, Pancuronium
Non-depolarising — benzylisoquinoliniumCompetitive antagonist; organ-independent breakdownAtracurium, Cisatracurium, Mivacurium
Why the chemical family matters

Aminosteroids ("-onium/-uronium") are reversed by sugammadex and depend on hepatic/renal clearance. Benzylisoquinoliniums are not reversed by sugammadex but atracurium/cisatracurium clear by organ-independent Hofmann elimination — the drugs of choice in renal and hepatic failure.

💥 3 · Suxamethonium — the Depolarising Agent

Fast on, fast off — and a long list of hazards

Dose 1–1.5 mg/kg IV; onset ~45–60 s, duration ~5–10 min. Its speed makes it the classic rapid-sequence induction relaxant. It is hydrolysed by plasma (pseudo)cholinesterase.

Phase I vs Phase II block
Phase I is the normal depolarising block (no fade on TOF, augmented by anticholinesterases). Repeated/large doses cause a Phase II block that behaves like a non-depolarising block (fade appears).

Complications to know cold

⚠ Hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest

Exaggerated K⁺ efflux via up-regulated extrajunctional receptors — avoid after ~24–72 h in burns, denervation/spinal cord injury, prolonged immobility and severe sepsis.

⚠ Malignant hyperthermia

Suxamethonium is a trigger (with volatiles). Masseter spasm may be the first sign.

⚠ Suxamethonium apnoea

Prolonged paralysis in pseudocholinesterase deficiency — quantified by the dibucaine number (normal ~80; homozygous atypical ~20). Management: ventilate & sedate until spontaneous recovery.

⚠ Others

Bradycardia (esp. second dose / children — pre-treat atropine), myalgia, raised intracranial, intraocular & intragastric pressure, transient hyperkalaemia even in normals (~0.5 mmol/L).

Choosing between the competitive blockers

📊 5 · Monitoring the Block — Train-of-Four

You cannot manage what you do not measure

A peripheral nerve stimulator (e.g. ulnar nerve → adductor pollicis) delivers four supramaximal twitches (2 Hz). Interpretation depends on the number of twitches and the presence of fade.

PatternMeaning
TOF count 0 (+ post-tetanic count)Deep/profound block — use PTC to gauge depth
1–3 twitchesModerate block — neostigmine reversal possible once ≥2 twitches
Fade (T4 < T1)Non-depolarising (or Phase II) block — the hallmark
No fadeNormal, or a depolarising Phase I block
TOF ratio ≥ 0.9Adequate recovery — the threshold for safe extubation
Residual paralysis

A TOF ratio 0.7–0.9 still impairs pharyngeal function and the hypoxic ventilatory response — clinical tests (5-s head lift) miss it. Confirm ≥0.9 with quantitative monitoring before extubation.

🔄 6 · Reversal

Two very different tools

A

Neostigmine (+ glycopyrrolate)

Inhibits acetylcholinesterase → more ACh to out-compete the blocker. Only works once recovery has begun (≥2 twitches / TOF count ≥2) — it has a ceiling. Give an antimuscarinic (glycopyrrolate/atropine) to block bradycardia, salivation and bronchospasm.

▼
B

Sugammadex

A modified γ-cyclodextrin that encapsulates rocuronium/vecuronium in plasma — reverses even profound block. Dose 2 mg/kg (moderate, ≥2 twitches), 4 mg/kg (deep, PTC 1–2), 16 mg/kg (immediate reversal after an intubating dose). Does not reverse benzylisoquinoliniums.

Caveats for sugammadex: binds hormonal contraceptives (advise extra contraception for 7 days), rare anaphylaxis, and needs a higher re-dose if rocuronium is given again soon.

🎯 7 · Special Situations

When the choice of relaxant changes

SituationApproach
Renal / hepatic failureCisatracurium / atracurium (Hofmann elimination, organ-independent)
Rapid-sequence inductionSuxamethonium 1–1.5 mg/kg, or rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg (with sugammadex available)
Malignant hyperthermia riskAvoid suxamethonium; all non-depolarisers are safe
Myasthenia gravisResistant to suxamethonium, very sensitive to non-depolarisers — titrate tiny doses with monitoring
Pseudocholinesterase deficiencyProlonged suxamethonium/mivacurium block — ventilate until recovery; dibucaine number confirms
Critical illness / prolonged immobilityAvoid suxamethonium (hyperkalaemia); watch for ICU-acquired weakness with prolonged NMB use
🚫 8 · Common Mistakes

Where it goes wrong

  • Paralysing before confirming you can ventilate/oxygenate.
  • Giving suxamethonium to a burn/denervation/SCI patient beyond the safe window → hyperkalaemic arrest.
  • Reversing with neostigmine at TOF count 0 — it cannot work with no twitches (use sugammadex or wait).
  • Extubating on clinical signs alone instead of a quantitative TOF ratio ≥0.9.
  • Forgetting the antimuscarinic with neostigmine → bradycardia/secretions.
  • Expecting sugammadex to reverse atracurium/cisatracurium (it won't).
🎓 9 · Exam Pearls — DNB / NEET-SS

High-yield one-liners

Q: Dibucaine number in homozygous atypical pseudocholinesterase?
~20 (normal ~80) — prolonged suxamethonium apnoea.

Q: Which relaxants are organ-independent?
Atracurium & cisatracurium — Hofmann elimination; drugs of choice in renal/hepatic failure.

Q: TOF ratio for safe extubation?
≥0.9 (quantitative).

Q: Sugammadex doses?
2 mg/kg (moderate), 4 mg/kg (deep), 16 mg/kg (immediate reversal).

Q: Muscle relaxants in myasthenia gravis?
Resistant to suxamethonium; markedly sensitive to non-depolarisers.

Q: Metabolite of atracurium that can cause CNS excitation?
Laudanosine.
