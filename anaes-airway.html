📘 1 · Miller's Anesthesia — Airway Management

Assessment, Prediction & the "Difficult Airway"

"Loss of the airway is the commonest route to anaesthetic catastrophe. The lesson of NAP4 was not that difficult airways are unpredictable, but that harm follows failure to plan for failure — persisting with repeated laryngoscopy, not calling for help, and delaying the surgical airway until hypoxia is irreversible. The safe anaesthetist assesses every airway, always has a Plan B and C, and treats oxygenation, not intubation, as the goal."

Synthesised from Miller's Anesthesia, 9th Ed — Airway Management; NAP4 (2011).

Definitions

  • Difficult facemask ventilation — inability to maintain SpO₂ >90% / provide adequate ventilation with a mask (predictors MOANS).
  • Difficult laryngoscopy — Cormack–Lehane grade 3–4 (only epiglottis, or neither epiglottis nor glottis, visible).
  • Difficult / failed intubation — multiple attempts, or failure to place a tracheal tube.
  • CICO — "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" — the emergency that mandates immediate front-of-neck access (FONA).
🔍 Bedside prediction — the bundle (see also the PAC page)
  • Mallampati I–IV, mouth opening ≥3 cm, thyromental distance >6.5 cm, 3-3-2 rule, neck extension, upper-lip-bite test, dentition.
  • LEMON for difficult intubation; MOANS for difficult mask; RODS for difficult SGA; SHORT for difficult FONA.
  • A documented previous difficult intubation is the single strongest predictor — always ask and read the old chart.
  • No single test is reliable alone; combine them, and always prepare for the airway you cannot predict.
💨 Preoxygenation & apnoeic oxygenation buy you time

3 minutes of tidal breathing (or 8 vital-capacity breaths) of 100% O₂ to an end-tidal O₂ >0.85–0.9 fills the functional residual capacity with oxygen and extends safe apnoea time. Nasal apnoeic oxygenation (15 L/min, or high-flow nasal oxygen) during laryngoscopy prolongs the safe window further — especially valuable in obesity, pregnancy and critical illness where desaturation is rapid.

📗 2 · Barash & Morgan/Mikhail — Techniques & Equipment

The Tools: Facemask → SGA → Tracheal Tube

"Airway management is a graded set of skills, not a single manoeuvre. Master basic facemask ventilation with airway adjuncts and correct positioning first — it is the rescue technique on which all the algorithms depend. The supraglottic airway is both a primary device and a lifeboat; the tracheal tube is the definitive airway; and videolaryngoscopy has become a first-line tool, not merely a rescue one."

Synthesised from Barash — Clinical Anesthesia; Morgan & Mikhail's Clinical Anesthesiology.

The ladder of airway devices

  • Facemask + adjuncts — head tilt/chin lift, jaw thrust, oro-/nasopharyngeal airway; two-person, two-handed technique for a difficult seal. "Optimal ear-to-sternal-notch positioning" (ramping in the obese) improves both mask ventilation and laryngoscopy.
  • Supraglottic airway (SGA) — LMA and second-generation devices (i-gel, LMA ProSeal/Supreme) with a gastric drain port; suitable for many elective cases and a key rescue device in the difficult airway.
  • Direct laryngoscopy (DL) — Macintosh/Miller blades; grade by Cormack–Lehane; optimise with external laryngeal manipulation (BURP), a bougie and correct positioning.
  • Videolaryngoscopy (VL) — improves glottic view and first-pass success, especially in predicted/unexpected difficulty and C-spine immobilisation; increasingly a first-line choice.
  • Flexible bronchoscopic intubation — the technique of choice for the anticipated difficult airway via awake tracheal intubation.
✅ Confirming tracheal placement

Sustained end-tidal CO₂ over several breaths is the single most reliable confirmation of tracheal (not oesophageal) intubation — "no trace, wrong place". Back it up with direct/video visualisation of the tube passing the cords, bilateral chest rise, auscultation and misting. Capnography also detects later displacement, disconnection and obstruction.

📙 3 · Stoelting / Benumof — Special Situations

Airways That Are Difficult by Context

⚠️ High-risk scenarios & how they change the plan
  • Full stomach / aspiration risk (emergency, obstetric, bowel obstruction, GORD, GLP-1 agonists) → rapid sequence induction, suction ready.
  • Obesity / OSA → ramp the patient, preoxygenate well, apnoeic oxygenation, anticipate difficult mask and desaturation; extubate awake, sitting up.
  • Obstetric → reduced FRC + increased O₂ demand (fast desaturation), airway oedema (use a smaller tube), higher aspiration risk — a leading cause of anaesthetic maternal morbidity historically.
  • Cervical-spine injury → manual in-line stabilisation, videolaryngoscopy; avoid excessive neck movement.
  • Head & neck / airway tumour, radiotherapy, infection (Ludwig's, epiglottitis), trauma/burns → consider awake tracheal intubation or a primary surgical airway; do not remove a partial airway with an anaesthetic you cannot reverse.
🇮🇳 Indian Context

A high burden of oral submucous fibrosis and oral cancers (tobacco/areca-nut chewing) produces restricted mouth opening and fixed, difficult airways — awake fibreoptic or awake video intubation skills are essential. Post-burn contractures of the face and neck and advanced airway tumours presenting late are common; plan for awake techniques and have surgical-airway backup and an ENT surgeon available. Ensure a difficult-airway trolley (bougie, SGAs, VL, cricothyroidotomy set) is checked and available in every location, including remote sites.

📋 4 · Guidelines — DAS 2015 & ASA 2022

The Difficult Airway Algorithms

DAS 2015 — unanticipated difficult intubation in adults DAS 2015
A stepwise, four-plan framework with a hard emphasis on maintaining oxygenation and declaring failure early:
Plan A — facemask ventilation & tracheal intubation: optimise position, preoxygenate, maximum 3 (+1 by a more senior colleague) attempts; use VL, bougie, external laryngeal manipulation. If it fails → declare failed intubation.
Plan B — maintain oxygenation via a second-generation SGA (max 3 attempts). Stop and think: wake the patient, or proceed via the SGA / intubate through it.
Plan C — if SGA fails, revert to final attempt at facemask ventilation (adjuncts, two-person). If adequate → wake the patient.
Plan D — CICO → declare, give 100% O₂, and perform emergency front-of-neck access (scalpel–bougie–tube cricothyroidotomy).
ASA 2022 Difficult Airway Guideline — key updates ASA 2022
Emphasises a pre-formed strategy, early consideration of awake intubation when difficulty is anticipated and the patient is at risk, combination techniques and videolaryngoscopy, limiting attempts to avoid trauma, ensuring supplemental/apnoeic oxygenation throughout, and having a clear extubation strategy. Human factors — call for help early, and use cognitive aids — are explicit.
Awake tracheal intubation (ATI) DAS ATI 2020
The gold standard for the anticipated difficult airway. Sedation (target a rousable patient, often remifentanil ± low-dose midazolam/dexmedetomidine), effective topical local anaesthetic (keep within safe lidocaine limits), supplemental oxygen, and ideally success on the first attempt. Verify tube position with capnography before inducing anaesthesia.
Extubation is a planned event — DAS 2012 DAS Extubation 2012
Stratify into "low-risk" (routine) and "at-risk" (difficult airway, surgical oedema, obesity/OSA, restricted access). For at-risk cases: fully reverse neuromuscular block (confirm TOF ratio >0.9), extubate awake and sitting up, consider an airway exchange catheter or awake fibreoptic-guided extubation, and have re-intubation equipment and a plan ready. Most airway complications in the peri-operative period occur at or after extubation.
💊 5 · Rapid Sequence Induction & Airway Drugs

RSI & Drug Reference

⚡ Rapid sequence induction (RSI) — the essentials

Preoxygenate → give a predetermined induction agent immediately followed by a rapid-onset muscle relaxant → intubate without intervening manual ventilation (to minimise gastric insufflation). Cricoid pressure is traditional but increasingly individualised — release it if it impairs the view or ventilation. Have suction on and to hand, and a failed-intubation plan pre-agreed before you start.

🗺 6 · Difficult Airway Algorithm

DAS Plans A–D at a Glance

Plan A — Intubate

Optimise position & preoxygenation. VL/DL + bougie, ELM. Max 3+1 attempts. Fail → declare.

Plan B — SGA

Insert 2nd-gen SGA to oxygenate (max 3). Stop & think: wake, or fibreoptic-via-SGA.

Plan C — Facemask

Final facemask attempt: adjuncts, 2-person, full relaxation. If oxygenating → wake up.

Plan D — CICO / FONA

Can't intubate, can't oxygenate → scalpel–bougie–tube cricothyroidotomy now.

1

Optimise the first attempt

  • Position (ear-to-sternal-notch/ramp), preoxygenate to EtO₂ >0.85, apnoeic O₂
  • Best device first (VL), best operator, bougie ready, ELM
2

Limit attempts, call for help

  • ≤3 (+1) laryngoscopy attempts — repeated attempts cause trauma & "can't ventilate"
  • Call for senior help & the difficult-airway trolley early
3

Rescue oxygenation

  • 2nd-generation SGA → then facemask with adjuncts
  • The question is "am I oxygenating?", not "am I intubating?"
4

Wake or proceed — a conscious decision

  • If oxygenating via SGA/mask: default to waking the patient unless surgery is immediately life-saving
5

CICO → front-of-neck access without delay

  • Declare CICO out loud; scalpel–bougie–tube cricothyroidotomy; do not wait for irreversible hypoxia
⚠️ 7 · Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes in Airway Management

❌ Mistake 1 — Repeated laryngoscopy attempts

Each extra attempt causes bleeding and oedema, converting a "can't intubate" into a "can't ventilate". Limit to 3 (+1 senior), and change something each time (operator, device, position, adjunct) — don't just repeat the same failing attempt.

❌ Mistake 2 — Fixating on the tube instead of oxygenation

The patient dies of hypoxia, not of an unplaced tube. Stop, oxygenate with SGA/facemask, and step back to think. Task-fixation is the classic human-factors failure documented in NAP4.

❌ Mistake 3 — Not confirming with capnography

"No trace, wrong place." A sustained EtCO₂ waveform is the only reliable confirmation of tracheal placement — auscultation and misting mislead. Undetected oesophageal intubation is fatal.

❌ Mistake 4 — Inadequate preoxygenation

Skipping preoxygenation (or a leaky mask seal) removes your safety margin. In the obese, pregnant and critically ill, desaturation is precipitous — preoxygenate to EtO₂ >0.85 and use apnoeic oxygenation.

❌ Mistake 5 — Delaying front-of-neck access

In CICO, waiting "one more attempt" until SpO₂ is in the 40s makes FONA a resuscitation, not a rescue. Declare CICO and cut early — the scalpel–bougie–tube technique is quick and reliable.

❌ Mistake 6 — Inducing an anticipated difficult airway asleep

Where difficulty and risk are predicted (airway tumour, fixed neck, severe OSA), an awake technique keeps the patient breathing. Don't take away a marginal airway with a drug you cannot immediately reverse.

❌ Mistake 7 — Treating extubation as an afterthought

Most peri-operative airway complications happen at/after extubation. Stratify risk, fully reverse block (TOF >0.9), extubate awake and upright in at-risk patients, and have a re-intubation plan.

🎓 8 · Exam Pearls — DrNB / MD / EDAIC

Exam Pearls

Q: Describe the DAS 2015 plans.
A = intubation (max 3+1 attempts); B = second-generation SGA oxygenation; C = final facemask attempt then wake; D = CICO → emergency front-of-neck access (scalpel–bougie–tube). Throughout: maintain oxygenation, limit attempts, call for help.

Q: What is the Cormack–Lehane grading?
Grade 1 full glottis; 2 partial glottis/arytenoids; 3 epiglottis only; 4 neither epiglottis nor glottis. Grades 3–4 = difficult laryngoscopy.

Q: How do you confirm tracheal intubation?
Sustained end-tidal CO₂ over several breaths ("no trace, wrong place"), plus visualising the tube through the cords, bilateral chest rise, auscultation and misting.

Q: What were the key lessons of NAP4?
Poor airway assessment/planning, failure to plan for failure, task-fixation with repeated attempts, delayed FONA, and events clustering at extubation and in ICU. Human factors matter as much as technical skill.

Q: Suxamethonium vs high-dose rocuronium for RSI?
Sux 1–1.5 mg/kg: fastest onset/offset but contraindicated in hyperkalaemia, burns/denervation and MH. Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg: comparable intubating conditions and fully reversible with sugammadex 16 mg/kg (a "can't intubate" rescue).

Q: When is awake tracheal intubation indicated?
The anticipated difficult airway at risk of failed asleep intubation/ventilation — e.g. airway tumour, oral submucous fibrosis, fixed cervical spine, severe restricted mouth opening. Confirm placement with capnography before inducing.

Q: What is the scalpel–bougie–tube technique?
The DAS-recommended emergency FONA for CICO: transverse stab through the cricothyroid membrane, turn the blade, rail a bougie into the trachea, and pass a size-6.0 tube over it.

Q: How do you extubate a high-risk airway?
Optimise the patient, confirm full reversal (TOF ratio >0.9), extubate awake and sitting up, consider an airway-exchange catheter, and keep re-intubation equipment and a plan ready (DAS Extubation 2012).
