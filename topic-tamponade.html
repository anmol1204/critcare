📘 1 · Washington Manual of Critical Care

Obstructive Shock From Outside the Heart

Cardiac tamponade is a form of obstructive shock in which fluid under pressure in the pericardium prevents the heart from filling. It is a clinical diagnosis supported — not made — by echocardiography: hypotension, distended neck veins and muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad) with a pulsus paradoxus in a patient with a pericardial effusion. What matters is not the size of the effusion but the rate at which it accumulated: a small effusion that forms in minutes (a stab wound, a wire perforation) tamponades, while a litre that collects over months may not. The treatment is to remove the fluid.

Summarised from the Washington Manual of Critical Care — Pericardial Tamponade.

Recognise it clinically

  • Beck's triad — hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds (often incomplete).
  • Pulsus paradoxus >10 mmHg — an exaggerated inspiratory fall in systolic BP; the bedside hallmark.
  • Tachycardia, raised JVP with a prominent x-descent, sinus tachycardia and low-voltage/electrical alternans on ECG.
  • Context — recent cardiac procedure/PCI/pacing wire, chest trauma, malignancy, uraemia, TB, post-MI free-wall rupture, aortic dissection.
📗 2 · Marino's The ICU Book, 5th Edition (2025)

Why Volume Helps and Vasodilators Kill

As pericardial pressure rises it is transmitted to every chamber, equalising the diastolic pressures and throttling venous return. The tamponaded heart is preload-dependent and rate-dependent: it survives on a full tank and a fast rate. Anything that drops preload — venodilators, aggressive diuresis, or the positive intrathoracic pressure of mechanical ventilation — can precipitate arrest. This is why the holding measures are intravenous fluid to raise filling pressure above the pericardial pressure, and why intubating a tamponade before drainage is so dangerous.

Summarised from Marino PL. The ICU Book, 5th Ed — Cardiac Tamponade.
🔬 The tamponade spiral
TriggerFluid accumulates in the pericardium faster than it can stretch

Impaired diastolic filling → ↓ stroke volume → compensatory tachycardia & vasoconstriction
Pulsus paradoxus (interventricular dependence)

📗 Marino — echo is the key, but don't be fooled by a small effusion
  • Diastolic collapse of the right atrium and right ventricle is the echo signature (RA collapse is sensitive, RV collapse is specific).
  • A plethoric IVC that does not collapse with inspiration confirms high venous pressure; its absence makes significant tamponade unlikely.
  • Post-cardiac-surgery tamponade may be from a localised clot compressing one chamber — the effusion can look small or loculated; keep a high index of suspicion.
📋 3 · Diagnosis & Drainage

Evidence-Based Management

Confirm with echo, but treat the patient ESC 2015
Urgent echocardiography confirms the effusion and tamponade physiology (RA/RV diastolic collapse, IVC plethora, respiratory variation in mitral/tricuspid inflow). But haemodynamic tamponade is a clinical diagnosis — in peri-arrest or trauma, do not delay drainage for perfect imaging. The ESC triage score weighs aetiology, clinical and imaging features to decide urgency.
Temporise: fluids & a chronotrope — never vasodilate STRONG
While arranging drainage: give IV fluid to raise filling pressure (most useful if hypovolaemic — less so if already high-pressure), keep the patient preload- and rate-dependent, and use an inotrope/chronotrope if needed. Avoid vasodilators, aggressive diuresis, and — where possible — positive-pressure ventilation until the pericardium is drained (intubation can precipitate arrest). If ventilation is unavoidable, use the lowest effective intrathoracic pressures and have drainage ready.
Drain it — pericardiocentesis or surgery definitive
Echo-guided pericardiocentesis (subxiphoid or apical) is first-line for medical tamponade — even removing a small volume produces a dramatic haemodynamic improvement. Surgical drainage (pericardial window/sternotomy) is preferred for traumatic haemopericardium, post-surgical clot, type A aortic dissection, and purulent pericarditis. In tamponade complicating type A dissection, do NOT do a routine full pericardiocentesis — relieving the tamponade can restore blood pressure and worsen the rupture; go to theatre (cautious controlled drainage only if peri-arrest).
Constrictive pericarditis — the chronic cousin
A rigid, scarred pericardium restricts diastolic filling without an effusion — right heart failure signs, raised JVP with prominent x/y descents, Kussmaul's sign and a pericardial knock. Echo/CT/MRI and haemodynamics differentiate it from restrictive cardiomyopathy (respiratory ventricular interdependence favours constriction). Definitive treatment is pericardiectomy; treat TB if that is the cause (a leading aetiology in India — see the Indian-context box below).
🇮🇳 Indian Context

Tuberculosis is a leading cause of large pericardial effusion, tamponade and constrictive pericarditis in India — suspect it in a subacute effusion with fever, weight loss and a lymphocytic/exudative tap; send fluid for ADA, AFB/GeneXpert and cytology, start ATT, and there is a role for adjunctive corticosteroids in some. Other common causes are uraemia (dialysis populations), malignancy, and post-viral pericarditis; trauma and post-procedural effusions are rising with expanding cath-lab access. Echo-guided pericardiocentesis should be available wherever there is echocardiography — the intervention is high-yield and life-saving. For suspected purulent (pyogenic) pericarditis, drainage plus antibiotics is essential.

🗺 5 · Clinical Flowchart

Suspected Tamponade — Recognise, Temporise, Drain

1

Recognise obstructive shock

  • Hypotension + raised JVP + tachycardia + pulsus paradoxus in the right context
  • ECG: low voltage / electrical alternans; consider it in any peri-arrest PEA
2

Confirm with bedside echo

  • Effusion + diastolic RA/RV collapse + plethoric IVC
  • Don't delay drainage for imaging in peri-arrest/trauma
3

Temporise safely

  • IV fluids, keep preload & rate up; inotrope if needed
  • Avoid vasodilators, diuresis, and PPV/intubation before drainage if at all possible
4

Choose the drainage route

  • Medical → echo-guided pericardiocentesis + pigtail drain
  • Trauma / clot / type A dissection / pus → surgery (in dissection, avoid full percutaneous drainage)
5

Find the cause & prevent recurrence

  • Send fluid for micro/cytology/ADA/GeneXpert; treat TB, malignancy, uraemia, infection
  • Watch for re-accumulation; NSAID + colchicine for viral/idiopathic pericarditis
⚠️ 6 · Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes in Tamponade

❌ Mistake 1 — Judging tamponade by effusion size

Rate of accumulation, not volume, determines tamponade. A small, rapidly-forming effusion tamponades; a huge chronic one may not. Look for the physiology, not just the fluid.

❌ Mistake 2 — Intubating a tamponade before drainage

Positive-pressure ventilation drops venous return in a preload-dependent heart and can cause arrest on induction. Drain first where possible; if you must ventilate, use minimal pressures with drainage ready.

❌ Mistake 3 — Giving vasodilators or diuretics

The tamponaded heart lives on preload. Vasodilators, GTN and aggressive diuresis reduce filling and precipitate collapse.

❌ Mistake 4 — Full pericardiocentesis in type A aortic dissection

Relieving the tamponade raises blood pressure and can worsen the aortic rupture. These patients need surgery; drain only cautiously if peri-arrest.

❌ Mistake 5 — Missing post-cardiac-surgery loculated tamponade

A localised clot can compress one chamber with only a small or loculated effusion. Falling output after cardiac surgery warrants echo and a low threshold for re-exploration.

❌ Mistake 6 — Forgetting to send the fluid

The tap is also a diagnostic opportunity — send for cytology, microbiology, AFB/GeneXpert and ADA to identify TB, malignancy or infection.

🎓 7 · Exam Pearls — DrNB / PDCC / IFCCM / EDIC

Exam Pearls

Q: What is Beck's triad?
Hypotension, raised JVP and muffled heart sounds — the classic (often incomplete) clinical triad of tamponade.

Q: What is pulsus paradoxus?
An inspiratory fall in systolic BP >10 mmHg — the bedside hallmark of tamponade (also seen in severe asthma/COPD).

Q: What are the echo signs of tamponade?
Diastolic collapse of the RA (sensitive) and RV (specific), a plethoric non-collapsing IVC, and marked respiratory variation in transvalvular inflow.

Q: Why do fluids help and vasodilators harm?
The tamponaded heart is preload- and rate-dependent; fluids raise filling pressure above pericardial pressure, while vasodilators/diuresis/PPV drop preload and cause collapse.

Q: When is surgery preferred over pericardiocentesis?
Traumatic haemopericardium, post-surgical clot, type A aortic dissection, and purulent pericarditis.

Q: Why avoid full pericardiocentesis in type A dissection?
Relieving tamponade can restore BP and extend the rupture — definitive surgery is needed.

Q: Leading cause of constrictive pericarditis in India?
Tuberculosis — treat with ATT; definitive treatment of established constriction is pericardiectomy.
