🎯 1 · At a Glance

What a Massive Transfusion Protocol Is For

A massive transfusion protocol is a pre-agreed, rehearsed pathway that lets a team deliver balanced blood components rapidly and without repeated ordering decisions during exsanguinating haemorrhage. Its value lies less in any single ratio than in removing delay and indecision: one phone call releases pre-defined "packs", the laboratory and porters know their roles, and the resuscitation team can concentrate on stopping the bleeding.

Summary of principles from the ISBT Clinical Transfusion Working Party (massive bleeding protocols) and national guidance.
🚨 When to activate
  • Actual or anticipated massive haemorrhage — e.g. ≥4 RBC units in 1 hour with ongoing bleeding, or bleeding likely to need replacement of one blood volume (≈10 units) in 24 h.
  • Use a physiological trigger, not just a unit count — persistent haemorrhagic shock (e.g. SBP <90, HR >120, a positive ABC score in trauma, or a raised shock index) with an uncontrolled source.
  • Common settings: trauma, major obstetric haemorrhage (PPH), upper-GI bleeding, ruptured aneurysm, and intra-operative surgical bleeding.
  • Activate early — one clear call to the blood bank; nominate a "team leader" and a "communicator" who liaises with the lab.
🩸 The goal: stop bleeding + prevent the "lethal triad"

Definitive haemorrhage control (surgery, interventional radiology, endoscopy, uterotonics/balloon in PPH) is the treatment; blood components buy time. Throughout, defend against the self-reinforcing lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy — plus ionised hypocalcaemia, sometimes called the "diamond of death".

📋 2 · The Protocol

Fixed-Ratio Packs & Component Targets

Empirical balanced ratio first PROPPR
Until laboratory results guide you, transfuse in a balanced ratio approaching 1:1:1 (RBC : FFP : platelets). The PROPPR trial found 1:1:1 versus 1:1:2 gave no significant 30-day mortality difference, but 1:1:1 achieved more early haemostasis and fewer deaths from exsanguination at 24 h. Include plasma from the first pack — rapid bleeding does not wait for coagulation results.

📦 Pack 1 (empirical)

  • ~4–6 units RBC
  • ~4 units FFP
  • 1 adult dose platelets (≈ pooled/apheresis)
  • Give tranexamic acid if within window

📦 Pack 2 onward

  • Continue 1:1:1 components
  • Add cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen if fibrinogen low
  • Switch to lab/VHA-guided targeted therapy as results arrive
Component targets to aim for
• Platelets > 50 ×10⁹/L (aim >100 in CNS/eye injury or multiple/ongoing bleeding).
• Fibrinogen > 1.5 g/L (aim > 2 g/L in obstetric haemorrhage) — replace with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate when it falls below ~1.5 g/L.
• PT/APTT < 1.5× normal — give FFP to correct.
• Haemoglobin ~70–90 g/L during active bleeding (don't over-transfuse red cells once bleeding is controlled).
• Ionised calcium > 1.1–1.15 mmol/L and temperature > 36 °C (see §3).
Whole blood & group selection
Start with emergency group O red cells (O-negative for women of child-bearing potential; O-positive is acceptable for males/older women to conserve O-negative stock) and switch to group-specific/cross-matched blood as soon as available. Low-titre O whole blood (LTOWB), where available, is increasingly used as a single-product initial resuscitation.
🧪 3 · Adjuncts

Tranexamic Acid, Calcium, Temperature & Viscoelastic Testing

Tranexamic acid — early or not at all CRASH-2 · WOMAN
Give tranexamic acid as early as possible, within 3 hours of injury (trauma — CRASH-2) or of onset of postpartum haemorrhage (WOMAN). Both trials showed reduced death from bleeding, and benefit falls with delay — after ~3 h it may increase harm in trauma. Dose: 1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 h (trauma); 1 g IV in PPH, repeated once if bleeding continues. It is cheap and widely available — do not omit it.
Calcium — the forgotten step
Citrate in stored blood chelates calcium, and rapid transfusion causes ionised hypocalcaemia that impairs both clotting and cardiac contractility. Monitor ionised calcium and replace (e.g. calcium chloride or gluconate) to keep it > 1.1–1.15 mmol/L during massive transfusion.
Temperature & acidosis
Keep the patient > 36 °C — use fluid/blood warmers and forced-air warming; hypothermia and acidosis each cripple the clotting cascade and platelet function. Correcting perfusion (haemorrhage control + balanced resuscitation) treats the acidosis better than bicarbonate.
Viscoelastic haemostatic assays (TEG/ROTEM)
Where available, use TEG/ROTEM to guide targeted component therapy after the initial fixed-ratio phase — directing fibrinogen, plasma or platelets to the specific deficit and reducing unnecessary transfusion. If VHA is not available, fall back on formulaic 1:1:1 plus standard coagulation tests and clinical judgement.
🛑 Deactivate & audit

Once bleeding is controlled, stand the protocol down promptly to avoid over-transfusion and its harms (TACO, TRALI, thrombosis, wastage). Return unused components, document what was given, and audit every activation — protocols work only if they are rehearsed and reviewed. Consider VTE prophylaxis once haemostasis is secure.

✅ 4 · Do's

What To Do

✅ Do — activate early on a physiological trigger

One clear call to the blood bank the moment massive haemorrhage is actual or anticipated — don't wait for the 10th unit. Nominate a team leader and a dedicated communicator with the lab.

✅ Do — give balanced 1:1:1 packs with plasma from the start

Empirical 1:1:1 (RBC:FFP:platelets) until lab/VHA results guide you — include FFP in the first pack (PROPPR: more early haemostasis, fewer exsanguination deaths).

✅ Do — give tranexamic acid early (<3 h)

1 g IV over 10 min then 1 g over 8 h (trauma); 1 g IV in PPH, repeat once. Earlier is better; it is cheap, safe within the window, and reduces death from bleeding.

✅ Do — monitor and replace ionised calcium

Keep ionised Ca²⁺ > 1.1–1.15 mmol/L — citrate load causes hypocalcaemia that worsens both coagulopathy and cardiac function.

✅ Do — keep the patient warm (>36 °C)

Use blood/fluid warmers and forced-air warming; hypothermia is a potent, reversible cause of coagulopathy.

✅ Do — chase definitive haemorrhage control

Surgery, interventional radiology, endoscopy, or uterotonics/tamponade in PPH — components only buy time. Use VHA (TEG/ROTEM) to target therapy once past the initial phase, and stand the protocol down when bleeding stops.

❌ 5 · Don'ts

What Not To Do

❌ Don't — resuscitate with crystalloid

Large-volume clear fluid dilutes clotting factors, worsens acidosis and hypothermia, and displaces clot. Give blood components, not litres of saline, in massive haemorrhage.

❌ Don't — delay tranexamic acid past 3 hours

The benefit is time-critical; given late in trauma it may increase mortality. If >3 h have elapsed since injury, weigh carefully rather than giving reflexively.

❌ Don't — wait for coagulation results before giving plasma/platelets

In the early phase, transfuse empirically in a fixed ratio; formal PT/APTT/fibrinogen take too long to guide the first packs.

❌ Don't — forget calcium and temperature

These are the most commonly neglected steps. Ignoring ionised hypocalcaemia and hypothermia perpetuates the bleeding you are transfusing for.

❌ Don't — aim for a "normal" blood pressure before control (in trauma)

In uncontrolled haemorrhagic trauma, practise permissive hypotension (e.g. target a palpable radial pulse / SBP ~80–90) until surgical control — over-resuscitation pops clots. (This does not apply to traumatic brain injury, where perfusion must be maintained.)

❌ Don't — leave the protocol running after bleeding stops

Continuing to transfuse causes over-transfusion, TACO/TRALI, thrombosis and wastage of a scarce resource. Deactivate promptly, return unused units, and audit the event.

🇮🇳 7 · Indian Context

AIIMS New Delhi & NBTC — Applying This in India

🇮🇳 Indian Context

Component availability varies widely. Many centres — especially district hospitals — may not stock apheresis platelets or fibrinogen concentrate, and rely on whole blood, random-donor platelets and cryoprecipitate. Adapt the protocol to what your blood bank actually holds and agree the local "pack" contents in advance with the transfusion medicine department. National guidance from the National Blood Transfusion Council (NBTC) and institutional protocols (e.g. AIIMS New Delhi) frame most Indian MTPs.

Tranexamic acid is a high-value, low-cost intervention that every Indian labour room, casualty and theatre can and should use early — the WOMAN trial was substantially conducted in low- and middle-income settings, including India. In obstetric haemorrhage (a leading cause of maternal death in India), combine early TXA with uterotonics, uterine tamponade/balloon and a fibrinogen target >2 g/L.

Practical priorities where resources are limited: activate early with a single call; use a fixed-ratio approach when VHA/lab turnaround is slow; never neglect calcium and warming (both essentially free); pre-arrange O-negative reservation for women of child-bearing potential; and build a rehearsed local protocol with regular drills and audit, since the biggest gains come from coordination, not exotic products.
