📘 1 · Washington Manual of Critical Care

They Drown, They Don't Bleed Out

Massive haemoptysis is usually defined by its threat rather than a precise volume — expectoration of blood (variously >100–600 mL/24 h, or any rate causing airway compromise or haemodynamic instability) that endangers life. The crucial insight is the mechanism of death: patients with massive haemoptysis asphyxiate — they drown in their own blood filling the alveoli — far more often than they exsanguinate. That single fact reorders the priorities: the airway and gas exchange come first, before the source, before the bleeding itself.

Summarised from the Washington Manual of Critical Care — Massive Hemoptysis.

Where does the blood come from?

  • ~90% is from the bronchial arteries — a high-pressure systemic circulation. This is why bleeding is brisk and why bronchial artery embolisation works.
  • The rest is from the low-pressure pulmonary circulation (e.g. PE with infarction, AV malformation, catheter-induced PA rupture) or the airway.
  • First confirm it is true haemoptysis — not haematemesis or a nasopharyngeal source (which change the whole approach).
📗 2 · Marino's The ICU Book, 5th Edition (2025)

Position the Bleeding Lung Down

The immediate danger in massive haemoptysis is spillage of blood from the bleeding lung into the healthy lung, drowning the one functioning unit the patient has left. The management therefore begins with gravity and isolation: put the patient bleeding-side down so blood pools in the already-compromised lung and spares the good one, give high-flow oxygen, and be prepared to protect the airway. When intubation is needed, the goal shifts from simply securing the airway to isolating the bleeding lung — by advancing the tube into the non-bleeding main bronchus, or with a bronchial blocker or double-lumen tube.

Summarised from Marino PL. The ICU Book, 5th Ed — Massive Hemoptysis.

📗 Marino — the value of knowing the side
  • Everything downstream depends on lateralising the bleed — position, lung isolation, embolisation and surgery all need to know which side (and ideally which lobe).
  • CT angiography of the chest is the best single first test in the stabilised patient: it localises the source, shows the culprit (often hypertrophied bronchial) arteries, and reveals the underlying disease — and it maps the road for the interventional radiologist.
  • Bronchoscopy both localises and can temporise (iced saline lavage, topical adrenaline/tranexamic acid, tamponade balloon), but a rigid scope in theatre is best for truly massive, brisk bleeding.
📋 3 · Localise, Isolate, Embolise

Evidence-Based Management

Airway & resuscitation come first STRONG
Position bleeding-side down, high-flow oxygen, wide-bore suction, two large IVs, cross-match, correct any coagulopathy/anticoagulation and thrombocytopenia. Give tranexamic acid (IV and/or nebulised) as an adjunct. If the airway is threatened, intubate early with a large-bore ETT (≥8.0) so a bronchoscope will pass, then isolate the bleeding lung. Do not wait for the patient to tire — lost airway in haemoptysis is rapidly fatal.
Localise: CT angiography ± bronchoscopy first test = CTA
In the stabilised patient, CT angiography is the preferred first investigation — it lateralises the bleed, identifies the culprit vessels and the underlying pathology, and guides embolisation. Flexible bronchoscopy is used when the side is unclear or to apply local haemostatic measures; rigid bronchoscopy in theatre for massive, brisk bleeding (better suction and control).
Definitive control: bronchial artery embolisation first-line definitive
Bronchial artery embolisation (BAE) is the first-line definitive treatment for massive haemoptysis from the bronchial (systemic) circulation — it controls bleeding in the great majority. Rebleeding is common (recanalisation, new collaterals, untreated disease), so BAE can be repeated and the underlying cause must be treated. Watch for the rare but feared complication of spinal cord ischaemia (anterior spinal artery arising from a bronchial/intercostal vessel). Surgery (lobectomy) is reserved for failed embolisation, or specific lesions (aspergilloma, AVM, trauma, localised resectable disease) in a fit patient — emergency surgery in active bleeding carries high mortality.
Treat the underlying cause
Common causes: tuberculosis and post-TB bronchiectasis, bronchiectasis of any cause, aspergilloma (mycetoma), lung cancer, necrotising pneumonia/lung abscess, and — less commonly — pulmonary vasculitis (e.g. GPA), mitral stenosis, PE, and iatrogenic PA rupture. Antitubercular therapy, antifungals for aspergillus, antibiotics for infection, and treatment of vasculitis are all part of definitive care once the acute bleed is controlled.
🇮🇳 Indian Context

Tuberculosis — active disease, and the sequelae of healed TB (bronchiectasis, aspergilloma in old cavities, Rasmussen's aneurysm) — is by far the commonest cause of massive haemoptysis in India. Always send sputum for AFB/GeneXpert and start/continue ATT; look for a fungal ball (aspergilloma) in an old TB cavity. Bronchial artery embolisation is widely available at tertiary centres and is the mainstay of control — arrange urgent transfer to an interventional-radiology-capable centre while keeping the bleeding lung down, oxygenating, and giving tranexamic acid. Where lung isolation expertise is limited, remember that advancing a single-lumen tube into the non-bleeding main bronchus is a life-saving, low-tech way to protect the good lung. Correct coagulopathy and stop anticoagulants.

⚠️ 6 · Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes in Massive Haemoptysis

❌ Mistake 1 — Focusing on blood loss instead of the airway

Patients asphyxiate on blood far more often than they exsanguinate. The airway and oxygenation, and protecting the good lung, come before everything else.

❌ Mistake 2 — Nursing the patient bleeding-side up

That lets blood drain into the healthy lung. Position the bleeding lung dependent (down) to spare the functioning one.

❌ Mistake 3 — Intubating with a small tube

A narrow ETT prevents bronchoscopy and lung toilet. Use a large-bore tube (≥8.0) and think isolation, not just intubation.

❌ Mistake 4 — Rushing to emergency surgery

Bronchial artery embolisation is first-line definitive therapy and controls most bleeds. Emergency resection during active bleeding carries high mortality; reserve surgery for failed BAE or specific lesions.

❌ Mistake 5 — Forgetting it is usually systemic (bronchial) blood

~90% arises from the high-pressure bronchial arteries — which is why bleeding is brisk and why embolisation, not "PEEP to tamponade", is the answer.

❌ Mistake 6 — Not treating the underlying disease

Rebleeding is common after embolisation. TB, aspergilloma, bronchiectasis, cancer or vasculitis must be treated to prevent recurrence.

🎓 7 · Exam Pearls — DrNB / PDCC / IFCCM / EDIC

Exam Pearls

Q: How do patients with massive haemoptysis die?
By asphyxiation (drowning in blood), not exsanguination — so airway and oxygenation take priority over the bleeding itself.

Q: What is the source of most massive haemoptysis?
~90% is from the bronchial arteries (systemic, high-pressure) — the rationale for bronchial artery embolisation.

Q: How do you position the patient?
Bleeding lung down (lateral decubitus) to protect the healthy lung from spillage.

Q: First investigation in the stabilised patient?
CT angiography of the chest — lateralises the bleed, identifies culprit vessels and disease, and maps embolisation.

Q: First-line definitive treatment?
Bronchial artery embolisation; surgery is reserved for failed BAE or specific lesions in a fit patient.

Q: A feared complication of BAE?
Spinal cord ischaemia from inadvertent embolisation of a spinal artery arising from a bronchial/intercostal vessel.

Q: Commonest cause in India?
Tuberculosis and its sequelae (bronchiectasis, aspergilloma, Rasmussen's aneurysm).
