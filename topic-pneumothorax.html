📘 1 · Washington Manual of Critical Care

From Benign to Instantly Fatal

A pneumothorax is air in the pleural space. Its significance ranges from a trivial apical rim on a chest film to the immediately lethal tension pneumothorax, in which a one-way valve pumps air into the pleura under rising pressure, collapsing the lung, shifting the mediastinum, kinking the great veins and abolishing venous return. Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis — a shocked, hypoxic patient with a silent, hyper-resonant hemithorax and distended neck veins — and it must be decompressed before a chest X-ray, not after.

Summarised from the Washington Manual of Critical Care — Pneumothorax.

Types & causes

  • Primary spontaneous — tall, thin, young smokers (apical bleb rupture).
  • Secondary spontaneous — underlying lung disease (COPD, TB, cystic/interstitial lung disease, PJP) — behaves worse; lower threshold to drain.
  • Traumatic — rib fractures, penetrating injury (± haemothorax).
  • Iatrogenic — central-line insertion, lung biopsy/aspiration, barotrauma from positive-pressure ventilation.
📗 2 · Marino's The ICU Book, 5th Edition (2025)

Why the Ventilated Patient Is at Special Risk

In the spontaneously breathing patient a small pneumothorax may resolve on its own; in the mechanically ventilated patient it is a different animal. Positive pressure delivered breath after breath can convert any pneumothorax into a tension pneumothorax, and it does so most readily in the very lungs that are already sick — ARDS, asthma with auto-PEEP, necrotising pneumonia. A sudden fall in compliance, a rise in airway pressures, worsening hypoxia and hypotension in a ventilated patient should trigger the thought "tension pneumothorax" and a look for it, because on the ventilator it evolves in minutes.

Summarised from Marino PL. The ICU Book, 5th Ed — Pneumothorax in the Ventilated Patient.
🔬 Tension pneumothorax physiology
TriggerOne-way air leak into pleura (spontaneous, trauma, barotrauma, line)
Progressive ↑ intrapleural pressure → lung collapse + mediastinal shift
Kinking of SVC/IVC → ↓ venous return → obstructive shock
ClinicalHypotension · hypoxia · absent breath sounds · hyper-resonance · tracheal deviation · distended neck veins → arrest
📗 Marino — decompress now, then drain properly
  • Immediate needle/finger decompression for tension — a large cannula in the 2nd intercostal space mid-clavicular line, or the 4th/5th ICS anterior-to-mid-axillary line (often more reliable, especially in adults with a thick chest wall). Finger thoracostomy in the ventilated/arresting patient is more certain than a needle.
  • Needle decompression is a temporising measure — it converts a tension to a simple pneumothorax; every tension needs a definitive intercostal chest drain afterwards.
  • On the ventilator, a tension can recur — do not remove the drain or relax ventilation vigilance until the leak has resolved.
📋 3 · Drainage & Troubleshooting

Evidence-Based Management

Tension pneumothorax — decompress before imaging STRONG
Do not wait for a chest X-ray. In a shocked patient with the clinical picture, perform immediate decompression (finger thoracostomy preferred in the ventilated/peri-arrest patient), then insert a definitive chest drain. In cardiac arrest with possible tension (especially bilateral in trauma), decompress both sides.
Who needs a drain vs conservative care BTS 2023
Management now weighs symptoms as much as size. A small, minimally symptomatic primary spontaneous pneumothorax can often be observed (conservative management is non-inferior in selected patients). Symptomatic or large pneumothoraces need intervention (needle aspiration or a drain). Secondary spontaneous, traumatic, and any pneumothorax in a ventilated or unstable patient have a low threshold for chest-drain insertion. Ambulatory devices are an option for selected primary cases.
Insert safely — the "safe triangle" safety
Insert the drain in the "safe triangle" — bordered by the lateral edge of pectoralis major, the anterior border of latissimus dorsi, a line at the level of the nipple (5th ICS), and the apex below the axilla — passing just above the rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle. Use ultrasound where available, aseptic technique, and confirm position (bubbling/swinging + imaging). Avoid the trocar-force technique; use blunt dissection.
Reading the underwater seal — bubbling, swinging, leaks
Swinging (fluid level moving with respiration) = the drain is patent and in the pleural space. Bubbling = an active air leak; bubbling only on cough/expiration is a small leak, continuous bubbling is a large/persistent air leak (bronchopleural fistula) — or a leak in the circuit. A drain that has stopped swinging and bubbling with a re-expanded lung is ready to consider removal; a drain that suddenly stops swinging may be blocked, kinked or displaced (do not simply clamp a bubbling drain — you can recreate a tension). Persistent air leak >3–5 days → seek a bronchopleural fistula and involve thoracics (suction, endobronchial valves, surgery). Suction is added for non-resolving pneumothorax/large leak per protocol.
🇮🇳 Indian Context

Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax from tuberculosis and post-TB destroyed lung is common in India, as is pneumothorax complicating necrotising pneumonia and (in the immunocompromised) PJP — these behave badly, often with prolonged air leaks and a low threshold for drainage. Iatrogenic pneumothorax from central-line insertion is an ever-present ICU risk — use ultrasound guidance and get a post-procedure check. Chest drains and underwater-seal systems are widely available and cheap; the key safety messages are insert in the safe triangle above the rib, never force a trocar, never clamp a bubbling drain, and always decompress a clinically-diagnosed tension before the X-ray. In trauma, remember the possibility of an associated haemothorax needing a larger-bore drain.

🗺 5 · Clinical Flowchart

Pneumothorax — Recognise, Decompress, Drain, Troubleshoot

1

Is it a tension pneumothorax?

  • Shock + hypoxia + absent breath sounds + hyper-resonance ± tracheal deviation/distended neck veins
  • Decompress immediately (finger thoracostomy if ventilated/arrest) — do not wait for X-ray
2

Stable pneumothorax — decide intervention

  • Small & minimally symptomatic primary → observe/ambulatory (BTS)
  • Large/symptomatic, secondary, traumatic, or ventilated/unstable → chest drain
3

Insert the drain safely

  • Safe triangle, above the rib, blunt dissection, aseptic, US where available
  • Connect to underwater seal; confirm position (swing/bubble + imaging)
4

Troubleshoot the seal

  • No swing → blocked/kinked/displaced; continuous bubbling → large leak/BPF (check circuit too)
  • Never clamp a bubbling drain; add suction for non-resolving PTX per protocol
5

Resolve & remove

  • Lung re-expanded + no bubbling/swinging → consider removal (per unit protocol)
  • Persistent air leak >3–5 days → thoracic referral (valves/pleurodesis/surgery); treat the cause
⚠️ 6 · Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes in Pneumothorax & Drains

❌ Mistake 1 — Ordering an X-ray before decompressing a tension

Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis. Waiting for imaging to confirm it costs lives — decompress first, image after.

❌ Mistake 2 — Trusting needle decompression as definitive

A needle only temporises (and can fail with a short cannula in a thick chest wall). Every tension needs a definitive chest drain; finger thoracostomy is more reliable in ventilated/arresting patients.

❌ Mistake 3 — Clamping a bubbling drain

Clamping a drain with an active air leak can recreate a tension pneumothorax. Never clamp a bubbling drain; investigate loss of swing instead.

❌ Mistake 4 — Inserting outside the safe triangle / using a trocar

Low or posterior insertion and trocar force risk injury to the liver/spleen/heart/vessels. Use the safe triangle, above the rib, with blunt dissection.

❌ Mistake 5 — Underestimating a pneumothorax in a ventilated patient

Positive pressure can rapidly convert any pneumothorax to tension. A sudden rise in airway pressures with hypoxia/hypotension is tension until proven otherwise.

❌ Mistake 6 — Forgetting the cause of a persistent air leak

A leak beyond 3–5 days suggests a bronchopleural fistula or ongoing lung pathology (TB, necrotising pneumonia). Escalate to thoracics rather than waiting indefinitely.

🎓 7 · Exam Pearls — DrNB / PDCC / IFCCM / EDIC

Exam Pearls

Q: How is tension pneumothorax diagnosed and treated?
Clinically (shock, hypoxia, absent breath sounds, hyper-resonance, ± tracheal deviation/distended neck veins) → immediate decompression before imaging, then a chest drain.

Q: Where do you decompress?
2nd ICS mid-clavicular line or 4th/5th ICS anterior-to-mid-axillary line; finger thoracostomy is preferred in ventilated/arresting adults.

Q: Where is the "safe triangle"?
Bordered by pec major (anterior), lat dorsi (posterior), the 5th ICS/nipple line (inferior), with the apex below the axilla — insert just above the rib.

Q: What does swinging vs bubbling mean?
Swinging = patent intrapleural drain; bubbling = active air leak (continuous = large leak/bronchopleural fistula or a circuit leak).

Q: Why never clamp a bubbling drain?
It can recreate a tension pneumothorax by trapping air with an ongoing leak.

Q: Which pneumothoraces have a low threshold for drainage?
Secondary spontaneous, traumatic, and any in a ventilated or unstable patient.

Q: Common Indian causes of secondary/persistent pneumothorax?
Tuberculosis/post-TB destroyed lung, necrotising pneumonia, and PJP in the immunocompromised — often with prolonged air leaks.
